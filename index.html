01 — The honest read

What the two-hour constraint actually buys

A weekly maintenance stop in a Keswick subdivision takes about 7 minutes in the yard plus 3–5 minutes of driving when the route is tight. Call it 10–12 minutes per customer, all in. Two hours a week is therefore a hard ceiling of roughly 9 or 10 customers — and that's counting the invoicing, the texting, and the "can you come Thursday instead" messages inside the two hours.

That is not a failure. Nine customers proves five things you cannot learn any other way: that Keswick will pay $110/month, that your stop time estimate is right, that people don't cancel in week three, that referrals happen, and that you can physically stand doing it. Every one of those is a number the $5,000 model depends on. Get them cheap, get them fast, then decide whether to buy more hours.

The real decision, deferred to week 12

You are not choosing between "small business" and "$5,000 business" today. You are buying data for about $150 and eight Saturday mornings. At week 12 you'll know whether the route is worth handing to a $22/hour scooper — and that hire, not more of your own hours, is what makes $5,000 possible.

Most of this market won't tell you a price

Three of seven make you fill in a form and wait; a fourth publishes only a $78–$350+ range. Publishing a plain, specific price card is by itself a competitive advantage in York Region — and it costs nothing.

Nobody is based in Georgina

Every operator runs out of Newmarket, Aurora or Richmond Hill. Keswick is the tail end of somebody else's route. "I live three streets over" is the entire pitch, and it can't be copied.

Don't chase the low end

Woof Waste Warriors' twice-weekly plan works out to $13.85 a visit; Poochy Poo's entry tier is about $18. Both are below the cost of your time once you count driving. Let them have those customers; price at $25 and win on being local and reliable.

The free first clean is your cheapest weapon

Poop Patrol charges $75 minimum for a 15-minute initial clean; Poochy Poo starts at $88 and others charge $95–$150. Giving it away costs you 30 minutes and lands in the customer's head as a $100 gift.

A 4.5× spread on one plan proves the tiering

Poochy Poo's weekly plan runs $78 to $350+ for the same service — the whole difference is yard size and dog count. That's the market telling you flat pricing is wrong. Tier by square footage from day one and you'll never have to renegotiate a big-yard customer.

Steal the photo and the gate

Poochy Poo advertises a photo of the finished yard and confirmation the gate was latched, on every visit. It costs ten seconds and it answers the two things every customer silently worries about. Put it on your flyer.

04 — Your price card

One rule you can quote from memory

$20 + $5 per dog + yard adjustment = price per visit

Yard adjustment is based on the area you actually walk, not the lot size on the listing. Small (under 2,500 sq ft) −$3 · Standard (2,500–5,000 sq ft) $0 · Large (5,000–10,000 sq ft) +$5 · Extra large (10,000 sq ft to ½ acre) +$12 · Over ½ acre, quote it in person.

A typical Keswick subdivision back yard is 2,000–3,500 square feet of walkable turf, which lands almost everyone in the Standard tier. Type the square footage into the calculator below and it picks the tier for you — after a dozen quotes you'll be sizing yards by eye.

Measuring it in 20 seconds: pace the yard heel-to-toe — one adult pace ≈ 2.5 ft, so paces across × paces deep × 6 ≈ square feet. A 20 × 25 pace yard is about 3,000 sq ft. Or drop the address into Google Maps satellite view before you leave the house.

The whole price card on one screen

Weekly service, per visit and per month. Screenshot this and keep it on your phone — it's every quote you'll give in the first year.

Why the frequency multipliers are what they are

Twice a week is not double the money — there's less waste per visit — but it is double the driving, so it prices at 1.85× the weekly rate, not 2×. Three times a week is 2.65×. Every-other-week costs 1.5× per visit because two weeks of accumulation takes half again as long. That last number is deliberate: every-other-week wrecks route density, so price it so weekly is the obvious choice.

The winter question, since you're starting in September

Snow doubles the time in a yard. Two workable options, both of which you should put to customers in early November:

Keep going at +$5 a visit, which is honest and keeps cash flowing through the quiet months — or pause until March with a pre-booked Spring Reset at $119, which locks them in, gives you a March cash spike, and hands you a warm list to reopen instead of a cold start. Most will pick the pause. Take the deposit.

The number to watch is minutes per stop

At 12 minutes a stop the model works. At 18 minutes — big yards, long driveways, a route spread across Keswick and Pefferlaw — the same 52 customers take 17 hours and the whole thing collapses. Route density is the business. This is why every flyer you drop should go on a street where you already have a customer.

06 — The plan

Twelve weeks, two hours each

Do these in order. Don't skip ahead to branding, a website, or a logo — none of your first five customers will care, and all three can be bought in an afternoon once money is coming in.

08 — Gates

Decide with numbers, not feelings

Each gate has a pass mark and a specific thing to change if you miss it. Missing a gate is information, not failure — the point is to change one variable and re-run, rather than grinding on a broken assumption.

End of week 2

Interest

≥ 10 genuine inquiries from ~60 targeted doors + 3 group posts.

If not — the offer is wrong, not the market. Lead with the free first clean in the headline and re-post.

End of week 4

Price

≥ 5 paying customers at the full $110/month.

If they only bite at $79 — Keswick's willingness to pay is below model. Re-run the $5,000 math before hiring anyone.

End of week 8

Unit economics

≥ 10 customers, ≤ 1 cancellation, average stop under 10 minutes, ≥ 3 five-star reviews.

If stops run 18 min — your yard tiers are mispriced. Fix the price card, not the effort.

End of week 12

Scale

15+ customers, referrals arriving unprompted, you're at the 2-hour ceiling.

If under 8 customers after 300 targeted doors — stop. Either extend to Sutton and Newmarket, or shelve it until the March spring rush.

09 — Housekeeping

The small number of boring things that are actually load-bearing

Everything here is either legally required or protects you from a bad day. Nothing else on the "start a business" checklists matters yet.

Insurance — before customer #1

General liability, roughly $600–$900 a year. You are entering strangers' back yards, near their gates and their dogs. One gate left open is the entire downside of this business.

HST — register voluntarily on day one

You're a small supplier until $30,000 a year, which you'd cross at about 22 customers. Registering later means raising every customer's price 13% mid-relationship. Register now, quote "+ HST" from the first flyer, and claim the tax back on gas, bags and gear.

Georgina home-based business

Home occupations are permitted in R1/R2 zones at up to 25% of the dwelling's floor area, but the bylaw says the business operates within the home — not the garage or a shed. Storing scoops in the garage is low risk; a five-minute call to Zoning (905-476-4301 ext. 2282) settles it.

Where the waste goes

Default: double-bagged into the customer's own bin — it's their household waste and it's free. Then sell "we take it away" at $10/month as an upsell, exactly as Woof Waste Warriors does. Do not put commercial route waste into your own curbside bin.

Boot and tool disinfection

Parvo and giardia travel on a scoop between yards. A spray bottle of dilute bleach and 20 seconds between stops. Then say so on the flyer — none of your competitors mention it and every dog owner who's had a sick puppy will read it twice.

Money hygiene

Separate chequing account or, at minimum, a dedicated e-transfer email from week one. Bill monthly in advance by e-transfer — it costs nothing, versus about 2.9% on cards, and it eliminates chasing payment entirely.
